Learn: glass relaxation, from experiment to model

Part I covers the experiments used to study structural relaxation: annealing, Kovacs jumps, DSC, enthalpy recovery and modulated DSC. Each one comes with a live simulation. Part II explains the mathematics of the three models and where they differ. TNM gives the whole sample one fictive temperature. MAP splits the relaxation into Prony components, and all of them return to the same temperature in equilibrium. The Takeda–Lucas model gives every domain its own fictive temperature, so the fictive temperatures stay spread out even in the equilibrium liquid.

1Fictive temperature and structural relaxation

Above the glass transition, a liquid's structure keeps pace with temperature. On cooling, the structural relaxation time \(\tau\) grows so quickly that the structure eventually cannot keep up and freezes. Tool described the frozen structure by a fictive temperature \(T_f\): the temperature at which the liquid would have this structure in equilibrium. In equilibrium \(T_f = T\). In the glass, \(T_f\) records the thermal history, which is why GARASU calls it the thermal fingerprint.

Enthalpy and volume follow \(T_f\). With liquid and glass heat capacities \(C_{p,l}\) and \(C_{p,g}\), the normalized heat capacity measured by DSC is

$$C_p^{N}(T) = \frac{C_p - C_{p,g}}{C_{p,l} - C_{p,g}} = \frac{dT_f}{dT}.$$

Relaxation towards equilibrium has three well-known signatures, and every model below has to reproduce all three:

  • Nonexponential. After a small temperature step, the response follows a stretched exponential (Kohlrausch–Williams–Watts, KWW), \(\Psi(t)=\exp[-(t/\tau)^{\beta}]\) with \(0<\beta\le 1\).
  • Nonlinear. \(\tau\) depends on the structure (\(T_f\)) as well as on \(T\). A glass therefore relaxes faster when it approaches equilibrium from above than from below (Kovacs asymmetry).
  • Non-Arrhenius. In equilibrium, \(\tau(T)\) follows a VFT, Adam–Gibbs or MYEGA form, and its steepness at \(T_g\) is the fragility \(m = d\log_{10}\tau/d(T_g/T)\big|_{T_g}\).

All three models describe a thermal history as a sequence of small temperature steps \(\Delta T_j\) and add up each step's delayed response. They differ in what relaxes (one \(T_f\) or many \(T_{f,i}\)) and in where each part relaxes to.

2Isothermal annealing (physical aging)

The simplest relaxation experiment starts in equilibrium, changes the temperature quickly to an annealing temperature \(T_a\) below \(T_g\), and follows a structure-sensitive property as the glass ages at \(T_a\). Tool used volume and refractive index. DSC, dilatometry, density and optical measurements are common today. When the changes are small, enthalpy, volume and refractive index all follow the fictive temperature, so every property gives the same normalized relaxation function:

$$\phi(t)=\frac{T_f(t)-T_a}{T_f(0)-T_a}\approx\frac{H(t)-H_\infty}{H(0)-H_\infty}\approx\frac{V(t)-V_\infty}{V(0)-V_\infty}.$$

If relaxation were linear, \(\phi(t)\) would be a single KWW function, \(\exp[-(t/\tau)^{\beta}]\), with the equilibrium \(\tau(T_a)\). In reality \(\tau\) depends on the current structure. After a down-jump the glass starts with \(T_f>T_a\), where relaxation is faster. As \(T_f\) falls, relaxation slows down. The measured curve therefore starts ahead of the linear KWW curve and ends behind it: it is stretched by nonexponentiality and nonlinearity. Separating the two is exactly what the models are for.

3Kovacs experiments: asymmetry and memory

Kovacs followed the volume of poly(vinyl acetate) after temperature jumps, writing the departure from equilibrium as \(\delta(t)=[V(t)-V_\infty]/V_\infty\approx\Delta\alpha\,[T_f(t)-T]\). Two of his experiments became the standard tests for any relaxation model.

Asymmetry of approach

Equal and opposite jumps, \(T\pm\Delta T\to T\), do not give mirror-image curves. After a down-jump (contraction) the glass starts with a high \(T_f\) and relaxes quickly, then slows down as it densifies. After an up-jump (expansion) the glass starts compact and slow, and it accelerates as it expands. Expansion therefore reaches equilibrium later. The asymmetry is a direct signature of nonlinearity, the dependence of \(\tau\) on \(T_f\).

Memory effect (Kovacs, Macedo–Napolitano crossover)

A glass is equilibrated at \(T_0\), quenched to \(T_1\), and aged just until its property equals the equilibrium value at a higher temperature \(T_2\). It is then jumped to \(T_2\). The average structure is now "right" for \(T_2\), yet the property does not stay put. It first moves away from equilibrium, passes a maximum (the Kovacs hump), and only then returns. Macedo and Napolitano found the same crossover in the refractive index of a borosilicate crown glass, and showed that it needs at least two relaxation times. The hump is larger when \(T_1\) is lower.

The memory effect shows that one number, the average \(T_f\), does not define the state of a glass. Some parts of the structure have relaxed further than the average and some less. At \(T_2\), the fast parts rebound before the slow parts finish relaxing. TNM encodes this history in its nonexponential memory kernel, MAP in its Prony components, and TL in its domain \(T_{f,i}\).

4DSC on cooling and heating

A differential scanning calorimeter measures the heat flow needed to change temperature at a constant rate \(q\), which gives \(C_p=\Phi/q\). Through the glass transition, \(C_p\) steps between the glass value \(C_{p,g}\) and the liquid value \(C_{p,l}\). Both baselines are usually fit linearly in \(T\), and the scan is normalized:

$$C_p^{N}(T)=\frac{C_p(T)-C_{p,g}(T)}{C_{p,l}(T)-C_{p,g}(T)}=\frac{dT_f}{dT}.$$

On cooling, \(C_p^N\) falls smoothly from 1 to 0 as the structure freezes. The frozen-in, or limiting, fictive temperature \(T_f'\) follows from the heating scan by Moynihan's area-matching method. Here \(T'\) lies deep in the glass and \(T^*\) in the liquid:

$$\int_{T_f'}^{T^*}\!\big(C_{p,l}-C_{p,g}\big)\,dT=\int_{T'}^{T^*}\!\big(C_p-C_{p,g}\big)\,dT\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad T_f' = T^*-\int_{T'}^{T^*} C_p^{N}\,dT.$$

On heating, the glass keeps its structure until \(\tau\) becomes short enough, and then catches up with the liquid. A glass cooled slowly (low \(T_f'\)) overshoots the liquid line: the enthalpy it lost is recovered in a peak. A glass cooled faster than it is heated relaxes first, which shows up as an exothermic dip below \(T_g\).

Slower cooling gives a lower \(T_f'\), and Moynihan and co-workers showed that the cooling-rate dependence carries the activation energy. The fragility follows from it:

$$\frac{d\ln|q_c|}{d(1/T_f')}\approx-\frac{\Delta h}{R},\qquad m\approx\frac{\Delta h}{R\,T_g\ln 10}.$$

The relation is approximate. In the simulation, compare the apparent \(m\) with the input value of 64.14.

5Enthalpy relaxation and recovery with DSC

Aging can also be followed with DSC. The glass is cooled to \(T_a\), annealed for a time \(t_a\), cooled back into the glass, and heated through \(T_g\). The enthalpy lost during annealing is regained on heating, so it appears as extra area under the \(C_p\) peak compared with an unaged reference scan:

$$\Delta H(t_a)=\int\!\big[C_p^{\text{aged}}(T)-C_p^{\text{ref}}(T)\big]\,dT=\Delta C_p\,\big[T_f(0)-T_f(t_a)\big],\qquad \phi(t_a)=1-\frac{\Delta H(t_a)}{\Delta H_\infty},\quad \Delta H_\infty=\Delta C_p\,\big[T_f(0)-T_a\big].$$

As \(t_a\) grows, the recovery peak becomes taller and moves to higher temperature. \(\Delta H\) rises roughly linearly in \(\log t_a\) and then levels off at \(\Delta H_\infty\), when the glass has reached equilibrium at \(T_a\). The model parameters are often fitted to a set of such scans, together with scans at several cooling rates, because the combination separates \(x\) (or its equivalent) from \(\beta\).

6Modulated DSC: complex heat capacity, reversing and non-reversing

Temperature-modulated DSC (MDSC, TMDSC), introduced by Reading and co-workers in the early 1990s, adds a small sinusoid to the underlying ramp:

$$T(t)=T_0+q\,t+A_T\sin\omega t,\qquad \Phi(t)=\langle\Phi\rangle(t)+A_\Phi\sin(\omega t-\varphi).$$

The heat flow is split into three signals. The total signal is the average heat flow, which is the conventional DSC curve. The reversing signal is the amplitude ratio. The non-reversing signal is what remains:

$$C_{\text{tot}}=\frac{\langle\Phi\rangle}{q},\qquad C_{\text{rev}}=\frac{A_\Phi}{A_T\,\omega}=|C^*|,\qquad C_{\text{nrev}}=C_{\text{tot}}-C_{\text{rev}}.$$

Schawe put the method on the footing of linear response theory. The modulation measures a complex heat capacity at frequency \(\omega\). The phase angle \(\varphi\) splits it into a storage part \(C'\) and a loss part \(C''\), and both follow from the relaxation function:

$$C^*(\omega)=C'-iC''=|C^*|e^{-i\varphi}=C_{p,g}+\Delta C_p\Big[1-i\omega\!\int_0^\infty\!\phi(t)\,e^{-i\omega t}\,dt\Big]\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{Debye}\ }\ C_{p,g}+\frac{\Delta C_p}{1+i\omega\tau}.$$

\(C'\) steps from \(C_{p,l}\) to \(C_{p,g}\), and \(C''\) peaks at the dynamic glass transition, where \(\omega\,\tau(T)\approx1\). GARASU computes the normalized \(C^*\) as the Fourier ratio of \(dT_f/dt\) to \(dT/dt\) at \(\omega\) in one-period windows, slid in steps of a tenth of a period and then averaged over one period, as instrument software does. The total is the one-period average of the same signal.

Caveats: why reversing and non-reversing are not "thermodynamic" and "kinetic"
  • The reversing signal is a dynamic quantity. \(C_{\text{rev}}=|C^*(\omega)|\) steps at the temperature where \(\omega\tau\approx1\), not at the conventional (fictive-temperature) \(T_g\). This step moves with the modulation period. It is not the equilibrium \(C_p\) of the liquid or the glass.
  • The non-reversing signal is not the enthalpy relaxation. Because \(C_{\text{nrev}}=C_{\text{tot}}-C_{\text{rev}}\), it inherits the frequency dependence of \(C_{\text{rev}}\). It mixes the enthalpy recovery of the underlying scan with the dispersion of the dynamic glass transition, and it changes with period and rate. It is nonzero even on cooling, where no recovery peak exists (switch to cooling below). Using a kinetic model of structural relaxation, Hutchinson and Montserrat showed how both components in the glass-transition region depend on the modulation conditions.
  • Linear response needs linearity and stationarity. Schawe's analysis asks for a small amplitude, several periods across the transition, and a phase angle corrected for instrumental heat-transfer lag. Under these conditions, \(C'\) and \(C''\) are the meaningful quantities. With long periods (400 s at 1 K/min, about 7 K per period), the sample changes within one period, and the separation becomes unreliable.

Part II

Models: three ways to describe a glass that is still relaxing

7Tool–Narayanaswamy–Moynihan mean field

TNM uses a single fictive temperature. Following Narayanaswamy, time is replaced by a reduced time \(\xi\) that runs at the current relaxation rate, and each temperature step relaxes with a KWW function of \(\xi\):

$$T_f(t) = T(t) - \int_0^t M\big(\xi(t)-\xi(t')\big)\,\frac{dT}{dt'}\,dt', \qquad M(\xi)=\exp\!\left(-\xi^{\beta}\right), \qquad \xi(t)=\int_0^t\frac{dt'}{\tau\big(T(t'),T_f(t')\big)}.$$

Nonlinearity enters through the Tool–Narayanaswamy relaxation time, where the parameter \(0

$$\tau(T,T_f) = A\exp\!\left[\frac{x\,\Delta h}{RT} + \frac{(1-x)\,\Delta h}{RT_f}\right].$$

Moynihan and co-workers discretized the integral for a history of steps \(\Delta T_j\):

$$T_{f,n} = T_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{n}\Delta T_j\left[1-\exp\!\left(-\Big(\sum_{k=j}^{n}\frac{\Delta t_k}{\tau_k}\Big)^{\beta}\right)\right].$$

Alternatively, the KWW function can be replaced by a Prony series, \(\exp(-\xi^{\beta})\approx\sum_i w_i\exp(-k_i\xi)\). Each term then updates recursively, which is much faster for long histories. The exponentials in this sum are a mathematical expansion of one relaxation function. They are not regions of the glass.

\(\Delta h/R\)
apparent activation energy. GARASU sets \(\Delta h/R = m\ln 10\,T_g\), so that the equilibrium fragility equals \(m\).
\(A\)
fixed by \(\tau(T_g)=100\) s
\(x,\ \beta\)

What TNM assumes
  • The structure is described by one order parameter, \(T_f\), and the whole sample shares one \(\tau(T,T_f)\). This is a mean-field description.
  • Nonexponentiality is put in by hand through \(\beta\), and it does not change with temperature.
  • In equilibrium, \(T_f = T\) exactly. The model has no spread of local structures, so it cannot describe the fluctuation \(\delta T_f\).

8MAP nonequilibrium viscosity with a Prony series (RelaxPy) Prony heterogeneity

The Mauro–Allan–Potuzak (MAP) model changes where \(\tau\) comes from. The relaxation time follows from the nonequilibrium viscosity through the Maxwell relation \(\tau=\eta/K_s\). In equilibrium, the viscosity follows the MYEGA equation, evaluated at \(T_f\):

$$\log_{10}\eta_{\mathrm{eq}}(T_f) = \log_{10}\eta_\infty + \big(12-\log_{10}\eta_\infty\big)\frac{T_g}{T_f}\exp\!\left[\left(\frac{m}{12-\log_{10}\eta_\infty}-1\right)\left(\frac{T_g}{T_f}-1\right)\right].$$

Deep in the glass the viscosity instead follows a nonequilibrium branch. It is Arrhenius in \(T\), and \(T_f\) sets the frozen configurational entropy:

$$\log_{10}\eta_{\mathrm{ne}}(T,T_f) = A + \frac{B}{T} - C\exp\!\left[-\frac{T_g}{T_f}\left(\frac{m}{12-\log_{10}\eta_\infty}-1\right)\right].$$

An ergodicity parameter \(y\) blends the two branches. It equals 1 in equilibrium and decays as \(T\) and \(T_f\) move apart:

$$\log_{10}\eta(T,T_f) = y\,\log_{10}\eta_{\mathrm{eq}}(T_f) + (1-y)\,\log_{10}\eta_{\mathrm{ne}}(T,T_f), \qquad y=\left(\frac{\min(T,T_f)}{\max(T,T_f)}\right)^{p}.$$

The KWW relaxation is written as a Prony series. A few exponentials with fixed weights \(w_i\) and rate factors \(k_i\) reproduce the stretched exponential accurately (Mauro & Mauro, 2018). Each term carries its own fictive temperature, relaxes towards the real temperature \(T\), and uses the relaxation time set by the global \(T_f\):

$$\exp\!\left[-\Big(\frac{t}{\tau}\Big)^{\beta}\right]\approx\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \exp\!\Big(-\frac{k_i t}{\tau}\Big),\qquad T_{f,i}(t_j)=T_j-\big[T_j-T_{f,i}(t_{j-1})\big]\exp\!\Big(-\frac{k_i\,\Delta t_j}{\tau(T_j,T_f)}\Big),\qquad T_f=\sum_{i} w_i\,T_{f,i}.$$
\(m,\ T_g,\ \eta_\infty\)
MYEGA parameters. By convention \(\eta(T_g)=10^{12}\) Pa·s.
\(A,\ B,\ C\)
nonequilibrium branch. GARASU sets \(A\) by continuity at \(T_g\).
\(p\)
ergodicity exponent. GARASU's default is \(p\approx0.308\,m\).
\(K_s,\ \{w_i,k_i\}\)
modulus in the Maxwell relation, and the Prony coefficients for a chosen \(\beta\)
What MAP assumes
  • The Prony terms can be read as regions that relax at different rates \(k_i/\tau\). After a fast cooling they freeze at different \(T_{f,i}\), so the glass does have a spread of fictive temperatures.
  • The weights \(w_i\) are fixed, and all terms share one \(\tau(T,T_f)\) set by the average \(T_f\). Nonlinearity therefore still acts in a mean-field way.
  • Every \(T_{f,i}\) relaxes to the same target, \(T\). In the equilibrium liquid the spread collapses: \(T_{f,i}=T\) for all \(i\), so there is no distribution of \(T_{f,i}\) in equilibrium and \(\delta T_f=0\).
  • \(\beta\) is fixed by the chosen Prony set and does not depend on temperature.

9Takeda–Lucas heterogeneous model spatial heterogeneity

The TL model starts from dynamic heterogeneity. The liquid is made of domains \(i\), and each domain has its own Vogel temperature \(T_{v,i}\), weight \(Y_i\), relaxation time \(\tau_i\) and local fictive temperature \(T_{f,i}\). The key step is that each domain relaxes towards its own equilibrium local fictive temperature \(T_{f,i}^{e}\), not towards \(T\). Only the average has to equal the real temperature:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{N} W_i^{e}\,T_{f,i}^{e} = \big\langle T_{f,i}^{e}\big\rangle = T. \tag{4}$$

Faster domains (smaller \(T_{v,i}\)) should sit at a higher local fictive temperature. In addition, Donth's fluctuation–dissipation theorem fixes the slope \(\big|\partial T_{f,i}^{e}/\partial T_{v,i}\big|_{\langle T_v\rangle}=T/\langle T_{v,i}\rangle\). The simplest relation that satisfies both conditions is

$$T_{f,i}^{e} = T\left(2-\frac{T_{v,i}}{\langle T_{v,i}\rangle}\right). \tag{8}$$

The weights come from the distribution of relaxation times. The KWW distribution \(G_{\mathrm{KWW}}(\ln\tau)\) with shape parameter \(f\), mapped through VFT, gives the Vogel-temperature weights \(Y_i\), and it turns out that \(W_i^{e}=Y_i\):

$$G_{\mathrm{KWW}}\!\left[\ln\frac{\tau_i}{\tau_{\mathrm{KWW}}}\right]=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{n!}\frac{(\tau_i/\tau_{\mathrm{KWW}})^{nf}}{\Gamma(nf)},\qquad Y_i=\frac{1}{I}\,\frac{1}{T_gD}\left(\ln\frac{\tau_i(T_g)}{\tau_0}+D\right)^{2}G_{\mathrm{KWW}}\big(\ln\tau_i(T_g)\big). \tag{11, 14}$$

Each domain relaxes with an Adam–Gibbs–Hodge relaxation time. Its nonlinearity comes from the average fictive temperature, and it has no extra parameter:

$$\tau_i = \tau_0\exp\!\left[\frac{D\,T_{v,i}}{T\big(1-T_{v,i}/\langle T_{f,i}\rangle\big)}\right], \tag{18}$$
$$T_{f,i}(t_j) = T_{f,i}^{e}(t_j) - \big[T_{f,i}^{e}(t_j)-T_{f,i}(t_{j-1})\big]\exp\!\left[-\Big(\frac{\Delta t_j}{\tau_i(t_j)}\Big)^{\beta_0}\right],\qquad \langle T_f\rangle=\sum_i Y_i\,T_{f,i}. \tag{16, 17, 3}$$

Two quantities follow directly. The first is the fictive-temperature fluctuation, which is linked to the enthalpy fluctuation. In equilibrium it is proportional to \(T\):

$$\delta T_f = \Big[\sum_i Y_i\big(T_{f,i}-\langle T_f\rangle\big)^2\Big]^{1/2}\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{equilibrium}\ }\ T\,\frac{\sigma(T_v)}{\langle T_v\rangle}.$$

The second is the instantaneous nonexponentiality. The weights of the relaxation times, \(X_i\), depend on \(T\) and \(\langle T_f\rangle\). A KWW fit of \(\chi(t)=\sum_i X_i\,\phi_i(t)\) therefore gives a \(\beta_{\mathrm{KWW}}(T,\langle T_f\rangle)\) that changes with temperature and with aging:

$$X_i=\frac{Y_i\,T}{L\,D}\left(1-\frac{T_{v,i}}{\langle T_{f,i}\rangle}\right)^{2}. \tag{22}$$

glass transition (\(\langle\tau(T_g)\rangle=100\) s) and fragility. Together with \(\tau_0\), they fix \(D\) and \(\langle T_v\rangle\) (Eqs. 23–24).
\(\log_{10}\tau_0\)
high-temperature limit of \(\tau\)
\(f\)

\(\beta_0\)

What TL assumes
  • Heterogeneity is spatial. Domains have different structures as well as different rates, and the distribution of \(T_{f,i}\) exists in the equilibrium liquid. Its width grows with \(T\), which gives a finite enthalpy fluctuation above \(T_g\).
  • On cooling, the distribution freezes. During aging it reshapes, and \(\delta T_f\) can evolve nonmonotonically.
  • \(\beta_{\mathrm{KWW}}\) is not an input. It follows from the \(X_i\) weights, so it changes with \(T\) and \(\langle T_f\rangle\). Nonexponentiality and enthalpy fluctuation have the same origin.

What to look for

  • At the start (equilibrium liquid). TNM and MAP have a single point: every \(T_{f,i}=T\). The TL distribution is already broad (\(\delta T_f\approx4\) K at \(T_g\)+40 K) and centered on \(T\).
  • During cooling. The MAP components separate as the slow terms freeze first, so MAP develops a spread only out of equilibrium. The TL distribution keeps its equilibrium shape until the slow domains fall out of equilibrium, and then it narrows as the fast domains keep following their targets.
  • During annealing. In both heterogeneous models \(\delta T_f\) first grows, because the fast components reach \(T_a\) while the slow ones are still frozen, and then decreases. Given enough time MAP collapses back to a single value at \(T_a\), while TL returns to its equilibrium width \(T_a\,\sigma(T_v)/\langle T_v\rangle\). At \(T_g\) − 20 K neither has finished within \(10^6\) s.
  • Change the cooling rate. Faster cooling leaves a wider frozen spread in both heterogeneous models. In MAP the Prony components freeze at more widely separated temperatures. In TL the distribution has less time to narrow during cooling, so it stays closer to its equilibrium width (for Se at the end of cooling to 288 K: \(\delta T_f\) ≈ 1.4 K at 0.1 K/min and 2.4 K at 100 K/min, against 4.1 and 5.9 K for MAP).
